MinFill and Tamaki have the same max rank 8 but Tamaki's tree is almost twice as deep (18 vs 10): one long chain of mid-sized steps. ND is shallow (7) but its top merges are expensive (rank 12). On a GPU, total work sets throughput, but the critical path bounds what can overlap and the step shapes decide efficiency. Same network, different plans, and no single number orders them.

Parallel bonds between two tensors are merged into one bond of dimension χ2 = .

\[ \text{order } \pi \;\longrightarrow\; \text{tree decomposition} \;\longrightarrow\; \text{pairwise tree} \qquad(\text{many-to-one}) \] here orders ↦ trees.
Eliminating an index merges the (current) tensors that carry it (carving.order_to_tree). Orders that differ only in the sequence of independent eliminations give the same tree, so the map is many-to-one: 5040 orders of the 7 indices of the 2×3 tensor grid, 208 distinct trees (data/S46.json). Generators that output orders or tree decompositions (MinFill, FlowCutter, the solvers) need this conversion; QXTools does it for the paper's pipeline. In circuits, two gates acting on the same pair of qubits produce parallel bonds; merging them is exact (dimension multiplies). The many-to-one map is also why the same width can hide very different plans (slides 21 and 45).

The paper's plan features, by block (Table 2)
\[ \text{step } i:\ \big(2^{m_i}\times 2^{k_i}\big)\cdot\big(2^{k_i}\times 2^{n_i}\big),\qquad c_i=m_i+n_i+k_i,\quad p_i=m_i+n_i,\quad k_i,\quad d_i=\frac{|m_i-n_i|}{m_i+n_i+\varepsilon} \]
Every pairwise contraction is a matrix product after reshaping: m legs only on A, n only on B, k summed. c is the work exponent (FLOPs ≈ 2^{1+c}), p the output size exponent (memory, output parallelism), k the reduction length (reuse, arithmetic intensity), d the shape imbalance: 0 for square products, near 1 for tall-skinny ones (arXiv 2608.05819 Eqs. 5–9, App. A.3). Plan-level features aggregate these over all steps, weighted towards the expensive ones (w_i ∝ 2^{c_i − c_max}), in five blocks. The bottleneck here (data/S47.json) is a square 8×32 · 32×8 product with a long reduction.

Learning to rank: score a group of plans, put the fastest first

our six plans for the talk's circuit · label from a FLOP proxy (χ = 2), not GPU time: \( Sp_i = 2^{L_{\text{MinFill}} - L_i} \)
\[ y=\log\big(1+\text{speedup over the MinFill plan}\big),\qquad \mathrm{NDCG@}k=\frac{1}{\mathrm{IDCG@}k}\sum_{r=1}^{k}\frac{2^{\,y_{(r)}}-1}{\log_2(r+1)} \]
One training example is a group: all candidate plans of one circuit with their labels. The ranker learns a score per plan from its features; only the order within the group matters. NDCG@3 rewards putting the best plans in the first three places. Training and testing split by circuit, so no circuit is in both (Table 3: 138 development, 25 test, 62 held-out QFT groups). Illustration (data/S48.json): labels here come from FLOP counts of our six plans, since we have no GPU timings; ranking by width already scores 0.959, ranking MinFill first scores 0.893 (Jdrasil, the cheapest plan, drops to second). The paper's labels are measured runtimes (50 runs on an RTX A6000, Eq. 12–13); it uses NDCG@3 as the validation metric with XGBoost's rank:ndcg (LambdaMART) objective but does not print the formula — this is the standard form with gain 2^y − 1.
