From treewidth heuristics to a learned ranker for GPU tensor-network simulation

One network, four tensors in a ring. By the end of the talk we will have seen seven ways to decide the order in which to contract it, and how arXiv 2608.05819 learns which of them runs fastest on a GPU.

Merging \(A\) and \(B\) with \(m\) legs only on \(A\), \(n\) only on \(B\) and \(k\) shared: \[ \text{step cost} = \chi^{\,m+n+k}, \qquad \text{result rank} = m+n \]
Same four tensors, same answer, two orders. Contracting neighbours keeps every intermediate at rank 2; contracting opposite corners first takes an outer product and creates rank-4 intermediates. Costs are with bond dimension χ = 2 and come from data/F04.json (carving.tree_stats): ((AB)(CD)) costs 8 + 8 + 4 = 20, ((AC)(BD)) costs 16 + 16 + 16 = 48. On real circuits the gap is exponential, not a factor of 2.4.

Contracting a bond = summing out one index

Tensor graph: tensors are vertices
Index graph: indices are vertices
Eliminating \(i\) joins its neighbours \(j\) and \(l\): the dashed fill edge is the new tensor \(T(l, j)\)
\[ T_{lj} \;=\; \sum_{i} A_{li}\, B_{ij} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \text{eliminate vertex } i,\ \text{connect } N(i)=\{j,l\} \]
Two pictures of the same network. In the index graph two indices are adjacent when one tensor carries both. Summing over i merges A and B into T(l, j); in the index graph, i disappears and its neighbours j and l become adjacent, because T carries both. That new edge is called fill. One click = one elimination.

\[ \mathrm{bag}_t=\{v_t\}\cup N_t(v_t)\ \ (\text{neighbours still present, fill included}), \qquad \width(\pi)=\max_t|\mathrm{bag}_t|-1 \]
Eliminating a vertex means: connect all of its current neighbours to each other (dashed purple fill edges), then delete it. Its bag is the vertex plus those neighbours (purple rings): everything that one step has to look at together. Same star, two orders. Hub first: the first bag holds all 7 vertices and the 6 leaves become a clique (15 fill edges), width 6. Leaves first: every bag is just {leaf, hub}, width 1. Width belongs to the order, not to the graph. For a tensor network the vertices are indices and a bag is the set of indices one summation touches, so a step costs χ^|bag| (next slides). Data: data/PR1_hub.json, data/PR1_leaves.json; tests/test_figures.py::test_primer_star_two_orders.

\[ \tw(G)=\min_\pi \width(\pi) \] Treewidth is the smallest frontier you must hold while taking the graph apart.
Four graphs, each eliminated in a best possible order, all advancing on the same click. Tree: peel leaves, every bag is an edge, width 1. Cycle: the first elimination must join two non-adjacent neighbours, so some bag has 3 vertices, width 2. 3×3 grid: sweep column by column; the live frontier is a column of 3, width 3. Clique: whatever goes first sees all other 4, width 4. Each shown width equals the exact treewidth computed by subset dynamic programming (traces/exact.py), so no order does better (tests/test_figures.py::test_primer_four_families_optimal). Intuition to keep for the whole talk: treewidth measures how big a frontier you are forced to carry; for tensor networks that frontier is the largest tensor you must store, χ^tw. The formal definitions with fill and tree decompositions come back in Act II.

Markov–Shi (2008). \[ \cc(G) = \tw\big(L(G)\big), \qquad \text{a circuit with } T \text{ gates is simulable in } T^{O(1)}\exp\!\big[O(\tw(G_C))\big] \]
Arnborg–Corneil–Proskurowski (1987). Deciding \(\tw(G) \le k\) is NP-complete.
Counting: n indices have n! elimination orders; n tensors have (2n−3)!! rooted binary contraction trees. Values from data/S05.json. The circuit is the talk's recurring network: a seeded 3×4 brickwork circuit with 8 cycles (traces/circuits.py). Markov and Shi: the best contraction complexity of a circuit's network equals the treewidth of its line graph, which is our index graph; simulation is polynomial times exponential in that width. Arnborg, Corneil and Proskurowski: deciding treewidth ≤ k is NP-complete. So exact search is out, and this is a heuristics talk. Bodlaender's linear-time algorithm for fixed k exists, but its constants grow so fast in k that it is impractical beyond very small widths. Sources: Markov–Shi, SIAM J. Comput. 38(3) 2008 (arXiv quant-ph/0511069v7): Prop. 4.2 cc(G) = tw(G*), G* the line graph; Thm 1.1/4.6 runtime with tw of the circuit graph G_C (the two differ by degree factors, Lemma 4.4: (tw−1)/2 ≤ tw(G*) ≤ Δ(tw+1)−1). Arnborg–Corneil–Proskurowski, SIAM J. Alg. Disc. Meth. 8(2) 1987 (k part of the input). See notes/theorem-facts.md.
