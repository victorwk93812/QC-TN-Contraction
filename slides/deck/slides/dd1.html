MinFill looks at every live vertex, counts the edges it would add, and takes the smallest. Ties go to the smaller degree, then a seeded random value. On the grid the four corners have fill 1 and go first; then vertices next to removed corners. Trace data/F06.json, seed 0 (the first seed in 0–9 reaching the best width). One click = one elimination. Three of the paper's seven candidates are in this family: MinFill itself (via QXTools) and two MinFill-based PACE 2017 entries by Jégou, Kanso and Terrioux (minfill_mrs: ties broken by a "neighbour future degree" rule; minfillbg_mrs: their H-TD-WT framework). Their PACE-specific refinements are not documented; present them as "MinFill plus tie-breaking refinements".

Why it is reasonable: on chordal graphs it never adds an edge

Step

A triangulated ladder is chordal: total fill , width = largest clique − 1.
Simplicial rule (Bodlaender–Koster–van den Eijkhof 2005). \( N(v)\ \text{a clique}\ \Rightarrow\ \tw(G)=\max\big(|N(v)|,\ \tw(G-v)\big) \)
Fulkerson–Gross (1965). \( G \) is chordal \(\iff\) \(G\) has a perfect elimination ordering (zero fill).
A vertex whose neighbourhood is already a clique (simplicial) has fill 0, and eliminating it first is always safe: it cannot hurt the optimum. Chordal graphs are exactly the graphs that can be emptied this way, and MinFill finds such an order because fill 0 is the global minimum whenever a simplicial vertex exists. So MinFill is exact on chordal graphs and a one-step-lookahead approximation elsewhere. Two eliminations per click on this slide (12 vertices). Data: data/F06_chordal.json (nx.is_chordal confirms chordality). Sources: Bodlaender, Koster, van den Eijkhof, Computational Intelligence 21(3) 2005, Rule 1; Fulkerson–Gross, Pacific J. Math. 15(3) 1965 §7 (the term "perfect elimination" is Rose 1970).

Width and cost disagree: MinFill is wider but cheaper, until L =

Width (memory exponent)
Total cost (time): MinFill minus sweep
\[ \log_2 \text{cost} \;\approx\; \max_t |\mathrm{bag}_t| \;+\; \log_2 \#\{t : |\mathrm{bag}_t| \text{ near the max}\} \]
Width is the exponent of the largest intermediate; cost sums over all steps. The column sweep hits its maximum bag L+1 at almost every one of its L² steps; MinFill's large bags come in a short burst at the end. So below L ≈ 22 MinFill is cheaper in total time despite being wider: at L = 20 log₂ cost 29.29 vs 29.51. From L = 22 on the sweep wins (32.12 vs 31.79), and at L = 24 MinFill's largest intermediate is 2^32 entries against 2^24 for the sweep: a 256× memory gap. Values: data/F07.json, acceptance tests for L = 8, 12, 16, 20, 24.

Yannakakis (1981). Minimum fill-in is NP-complete; MinFill is its one-step greedy approximation.
Tie-breaking alone moves the width from 26 to 30 across 40 seeds at L = 20 (median 28). The rule decides from distance-2 information: each update only touches vertices within distance 2 of the eliminated one, O(|U|Δ²), but Δ grows to about the width once fill accumulates. The bag profile shows the late sequential chain: the expensive steps all come at the end, one after another, which is bad for GPU parallelism (Act IV). On a random cubic graph (n = 320, graph seed 1, 20 tie seeds) MinFill is strong: best 56, median 59.5, against MinDegree median 62 (best 59); nested dissection with our separator finders gives 57 (spectral), 64 (FlowCutter-lite), 67 (BFS levels), 78 (Kernighan–Lin, seed 0) — data/S25.json.
